MONEY, MARKETS AND THE BIG PICTURE

WHEN THE DRAGON STUMBLES

China's market crash: where did the money go?

By Prakarshi Polina October 29, 2026 7 min read

Imagine waking up to a headline saying that trillions of dollars have disappeared from the Chinese stock market. Where did that money go? Did someone take it? Did companies suddenly lose all their cash? Or is there something about the stock market that most of us never really understood?

First, an important distinction: a fall in the value of a stock market does not mean the same amount of cash has physically vanished. It usually means investors are now willing to pay less for the shares they own.

What happened to China's stock market?

Chinese stocks have experienced significant market volatility. Technology shares, including companies connected to semiconductors and artificial intelligence, have faced pressure as investors reassess their expectations for future growth.

A market decline can unfold over days or months. Headlines about market value lost since a peak often describe a cumulative fall, not necessarily money lost during one trading session. Exact figures depend on the index, the dates being compared, and the currency used.

Why do stock prices fall?

A stock price reflects what buyers are willing to pay and what sellers are willing to accept. When investors become less optimistic about a company's future profits, they may sell their shares. If buyers will only pay a lower price, the share price falls.

Several factors can contribute to a market decline:

  • High expectations: investors may have priced in rapid growth that companies struggle to deliver.
  • Economic uncertainty: worries about consumer spending, business growth, or profits can weaken confidence.
  • Technology competition: companies can face pressure from competitors, changing regulations, and costly investment.
  • Investor behaviour: when prices start falling, fear can prompt more selling and intensify the decline.

How can trillions disappear without cash vanishing?

Imagine a company has one million shares, each trading at ₹100. Its market value is ₹10 crore.

Now imagine investors become worried and the share price falls to ₹60. The same one million shares are now valued at ₹6 crore.

THE SIMPLE MATH

Before: 1,000,000 shares × ₹100 = ₹10 crore.

After: 1,000,000 shares × ₹60 = ₹6 crore.

Change in market value: minus ₹4 crore.

This is a reduction in the market's valuation of the shares. It does not mean ₹4 crore was necessarily withdrawn from the company's bank account.

A stock market's total value is calculated using share prices. Those prices can change even though every shareholder has not sold their shares. When the price drops, the value assigned to the shares still being held also drops.

So who actually loses money?

If you buy a share for ₹100 and its price falls to ₹60, your investment is now worth ₹60. You have an unrealised loss of ₹40 per share. If you sell at ₹60, that loss becomes realised.

Someone who bought at ₹50 and sells at ₹60 could still make a profit. The same market decline affects investors differently depending on when they bought, when they sold, and how much risk they took.

  • Shareholders: their investments lose value when share prices fall.
  • People who sell at a loss: they lock in the loss compared with their purchase price.
  • Borrowed-money investors: leverage can magnify losses and may trigger forced selling.
  • Companies: their market valuations fall, although their cash reserves do not automatically fall by the same amount.

Why does everyone start selling at once?

Markets are influenced by expectations and emotions as well as financial results. When investors see prices falling, some sell because they fear bigger losses. Other investors reduce risk because they have borrowed money or must meet financial obligations.

This can create a feedback loop: prices fall, more people sell, and prices fall further. But markets can also recover when confidence returns or investors decide prices have become attractive.

Could this affect India?

A sharp decline in China can influence other markets, but it does not automatically mean India's stock market will crash. Investors may reassess risk across countries, global technology shares may be affected by changing expectations, and slower Chinese demand can influence companies that depend on exports or commodities.

The actual impact depends on the cause of the decline, the connection between markets, currency movements, investor positioning, and the policy response. These are possible spillovers, not a guaranteed prediction.

What should ordinary investors learn from this?

  • A company's share price can fall even if the business still operates.
  • A large market-value loss is not the same as an equal amount of cash disappearing.
  • Borrowing to invest can make a market decline much more dangerous.
  • Excitement about a technology does not guarantee that every company in that industry is fairly valued.
  • Headlines should be checked for the index, time period, and source behind their numbers.

The takeaway

Think of the stock market as a giant auction. When investors expect better profits, they may bid share prices higher. When they become worried, they may offer less. The market value changes as those expectations change.

So when you hear that billions or trillions have disappeared, ask a simple question: are we talking about cash that was lost, or the value investors currently assign to their shares? The difference matters.

A NOTE ON THE NEWS

Market prices and reported losses change frequently. Before publishing a specific claim about a recent Chinese sell-off, verify the index, date range, percentage decline, and market value figure against current reporting. This explainer describes how market-value losses work; it does not verify every headline figure about the latest decline.
